# Pedagogical Statement

I have taught mathematics to secondary school students in Nigeria, trained and coached teachers in public and private schools, supported doctoral seminars in comparative education in Beijing, and helped deliver graduate courses in higher education administration in Alabama. Across these very different settings, one conviction has guided my work: every learner can grow when teaching is purposeful, inclusive and responsive to evidence. I see teaching not as the transfer of information, but as the design of experiences that help people make meaning, connect ideas to their own lives and contexts, and build the confidence to keep learning. Four principles shape how I teach and how I support other teachers.

> Every learner can grow when teaching is purposeful, inclusive and responsive to evidence.

## 1. Learning begins with belonging

Students learn best when they feel that they belong and that their experiences matter. I came to this understanding as an international student in China and the United States, where I saw how much a clear expectation, an accessible resource or a teacher's willingness to listen could change a student's path. In Beijing, I facilitated doctoral and master's seminars with students from many countries, and I learned to invite comparisons drawn from students' own educational backgrounds and systems so that every voice became a resource for the class. As President of the International Students Association in the Faculty of Education, I worked to make library resources accessible in English and founded a conference where students and faculty could exchange research as colleagues.

In my own classes, I establish clear norms for respectful discussion, learn students' names and backgrounds early, use varied ways for students to participate, and make expectations and grading criteria transparent so that no one is disadvantaged by unwritten rules.

## 2. Clear goals, aligned activities and fair assessment

I plan courses by starting with what students already know and what they should be able to do at the end, then designing activities and assessments that lead there. This approach guides my work as School Director of Victory Delight College, where I lead the design and review of the curriculum and help teachers align learning outcomes, lesson activities and assessments with national standards and examination requirements. It also shapes my contribution to AHE 500: Perspectives in Higher Education Administration at The University of Alabama, where I take part in course planning and instructional meetings focused on student learning outcomes.

I value assessments that are varied and meaningful, such as case analyses, data projects, policy briefs, presentations and reflective writing, so that students can show their learning in more than one way and practice the kinds of work they will do in their careers.

## 3. Active, learner-centered learning connected to real problems

I believe students learn most deeply when they actively work with ideas in ways that strengthen their critical thinking. In graduate seminars, I use structured discussion and activities, comparative case studies and small-group analysis of real policy problems. When teaching research methods and data use, I favor hands-on practice: in a workshop I designed for graduate students at Alabama, participants retrieved, cleaned and analyzed national higher education data themselves and applied it to their own theses and projects.

As a mathematics teacher, I designed lessons and assessments for students of different ability levels, and as a teacher trainer I encouraged learner-centered and activity-based methods that help teachers adapt instruction to students' different needs. I connect course content to issues students care about, such as college affordability, access and opportunity, so that learning feels relevant beyond the classroom.

## 4. Feedback, reflection and evidence drive improvement

Good teaching improves through feedback, for students and for teachers. I give students timely, specific feedback that explains not only what to improve but how, and I build in chances for revision and reflection. I apply the same principle to my own teaching and to my work with other educators. As a School Facilitator and Inspector for Nigeria's Universal Basic Education program, I trained more than 60 teachers through professional development workshops, observed about 20 teachers' classrooms, and gave each individual coaching and feedback, then used what I learned from observation to plan the next round of training. I continue this cycle of observation, feedback and follow-up with the teachers at Victory Delight College.

As a researcher, I also value evidence about teaching: I contributed to UNESCO's International Science and Evidence-Based Education Assessment, including chapters on the teaching profession and on assessment, and I have studied teacher beliefs, teacher preparation and professional learning communities. I seek feedback on my own teaching through student input and conversations with colleagues, and I adjust my courses accordingly.

## Supporting other teachers

Some of my most rewarding work has been helping other educators grow. I approach professional development the way I approach teaching: I start from teachers' real classroom challenges, model the strategies I recommend, give participants time to practice and plan, and follow up through observation and coaching. When training teachers in Ibadan North Local Government Area, I introduced readings, such as Tim LaHaye's *Why You Act the Way You Do*, to help teachers reflect on temperament and behaviour and strengthen their relationships with colleagues, parents and students. I believe that faculty, like students, learn best in communities where they can experiment, share what works and examine evidence together.

## Looking ahead

Whether I am teaching a graduate course, facilitating a workshop or consulting with an instructor about a course design, my goal is the same: to create learning environments where all learners are respected, challenged and supported to succeed. I am committed to continuing to learn about inclusive pedagogy and faculty development, to studying my own practice through the scholarship of teaching and learning, and to helping colleagues build courses in which every student has a fair opportunity to thrive.
